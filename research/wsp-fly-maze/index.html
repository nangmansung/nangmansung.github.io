Can a language model program a fly brain directly?

Weight-space programming on the FlyWire connectome. Claude Opus 5.5 edited 0.01% of synapses and raised escape from unseen mazes from about 4% to 100%.

1. Overview

A topic called weight-space programming has caught my eye lately. The study came out around November 3, 2026 (Korean time). In short, a frontier language model edited the weights of small AI models directly and made them perform better. For example, it changed just 0.016% of the weights of the small language model Qwen3.5-0.8B and raised its score on math problems (MATH-500) from 7.9% to 48.1%.

Until now, neural networks have mostly been improved with learning algorithms. Ordinary networks are trained with gradient descent; spiking neural networks (SNNs) often use learning rules such as STDP. Either way, the weights are nudged step by step, following a fixed rule, in whatever direction improves performance, and nobody needs to know what any single weight means. This study shows a different path: a language model reads a network the way an engineer reads code, works out why it fails, and edits only the weights that matter.

Personally, this felt like "AI's first step into the realm of the gods." Imagine someone announcing that they had rewired a frog's brain by hand and made the frog smarter. Before even getting to the ethics, people might say we had truly stepped into that realm.

So I wondered what would happen if we applied this to the brain of a real animal. We cannot try it on a living animal, so I tried it on the fruit fly brain, whose complete wiring diagram was recently published.

2. Methods

The brain

  • Wiring diagram: I downloaded the FlyWire whole-brain connectome of the fruit fly (release 783). It maps every neuron and synapse of an adult fly brain from electron microscopy.
  • Neuron model: I used the whole-brain fly model of Shiu et al. (2024, Nature) unchanged: 138,639 neurons and about 15 million connections.
    • Every neuron is the same simple LIF (leaky integrate-and-fire) unit. Input raises its voltage, the voltage slowly leaks away, and when it crosses a threshold the neuron fires.
    • The only thing that differs between neurons is the connection matrix W. Each entry of W is the number of synapses between two neurons, with an excitatory (+) or inhibitory (−) sign set by the neurotransmitter.
    • There are no learned parameters at all. To change what this brain does, you have to change W.

Body and maze

I attached a very simple body to the brain.

  • The fly walks constantly at 10 mm/s through a grid maze of 5 mm cells. It starts in one corner; reaching the opposite corner counts as an escape.
  • Every 5 ms the brain and the body exchange three signals.
    • Heading input: among the compass neurons (EPG), the ones that match the current heading are stimulated.
    • Goal input: among the goal-direction neurons (FC2), the ones that match "the direction of the next cell on the shortest path" are stimulated.
    • Steering output: the turn rate is set by the difference in firing between the left and right DNa02 neurons. Turn rate = −5 × (right − left) deg/s.

But the original brain turned left almost all the time, at about 90 degrees per second, wherever the goal was. So the flies circled in place and only about 4% escaped.

The AI editor

In the original study, a frontier model edited the weights directly. Does the same approach work on a fly brain? This time I had Claude Code (Claude Opus 5.5) edit W directly, under these rules:

  • Only existing connections can change. No new connections.
  • The excitatory/inhibitory sign cannot change.
  • A connection can grow to at most twice its original strength.
  • At most 1% of all connections can change.

The editor had a Python environment for analysing the wiring and a tool for running simulations. The key condition was how many simulations it could run: 0 (wiring diagram only), 10 or 50.

After editing, the original and edited brains were compared on 16 mazes the editor had never seen. The same 128 flies with the same random noise were used for both brains, so every fly is a paired comparison.

  • 0 simulations: all three editors failed. All three spotted a conspicuous left/right asymmetry in the wiring of a neuron type called AOTU019 and "fixed" it, with no effect. Later, even switching AOTU019 off entirely left the left bias in place. A plausible explanation drawn from the wiring alone was wrong.
  • 10 simulations: all three improved escape a lot (+30 to +78 points). They fixed different things: two balanced the left and right PFL3 neurons, which compare heading with goal, while the best one adjusted the connections onto the steering neurons DNa02.
  • 50 simulations: both editors got almost every fly out (128 and 125 of 128). Both identified a left/right imbalance in PFL3 as the cause: the goal neurons (FC2) send fewer synapses to left PFL3 than to right PFL3, and the circuit downstream amplifies this small difference into a state where "left always wins." The best edit changed only 542 connections, 0.01% of all synapses.
  • Controls without AI: ten random edits of the same size (−2.3 to +2.3 points) and an edit that made every left/right-asymmetric cell type symmetric by rule (−3.1 points) did nothing. Applying the AI's kind of edit to the wrong hemisphere more than doubled left turning. The effect came not from changing connections, but from knowing which ones to change.

4. Limitations

The program told the brain where the goal was

The program knows the whole maze. Every 5 ms it computes the shortest path from the current cell to the exit and sets the goal direction toward the centre of the next cell on that path. It then stimulates the FC2 neurons for that direction at 150 Hz. In effect, it tells the brain "go this way."

This is what "local beacon" means in the methods. Picture a small flag in the next cell; when the fly gets there, the flag moves on to the cell after.

Why FC2

In real flies, FC2 neurons are thought to represent the direction the fly wants to go (its goal direction). A real fly sets this direction from memory or learning. PFL3 neurons then compare the current heading (EPG) with the goal direction (FC2) and decide which way to turn. This experiment skips the step where a real fly chooses its goal and feeds a ready-made goal direction straight into FC2.

Heading works the same way. The program knows the fly's true heading and stimulates the matching compass neurons (EPG). This corresponds to a fly knowing its heading from a visible landmark.

So what did this experiment test?

All the brain had to do was "turn toward the direction it is given." Route finding and planning were done by the program. Yet the original brain could not even do this simple job: it kept turning left even when the goal was on its right. What the AI repaired is this turn-toward-goal reflex.

Other limitations

  • Few runs. Each condition was run only 2–3 times. The direction of the result is clear, but runs vary a lot.
  • A model, not a fly. All neurons in this model are simple point neurons, with no synaptic plasticity or neuromodulation. The left bias may be a property of the simplified model rather than of real flies. The PFL3 wiring asymmetry is a prediction worth testing, not a biological finding.
